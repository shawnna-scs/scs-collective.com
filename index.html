The Brand Voice Trap: Why Consistency Alone Won't Save You

Pull up any brand's style guide and you'll find the same promises: we're bold but approachable, professional but human, clear but not cold. The words are different. The intention is identical. And somewhere in the gap between the document and the work, the voice disappears.

Consistency is not a voice. It's a container. You can be consistently dull. You can be consistently forgettable. You can follow every rule in your guide and still produce content that slides past people without leaving a mark.

What actually builds a brand voice is a point of view. Not a tone. Not a list of adjectives. A genuine perspective on something that matters to your audience. A willingness to say the thing your competitors won't. A reason for people to feel, even briefly, like they found someone who gets it.

I've worked with brands at every size, and the ones with the strongest voices share one thing: they made a decision about what they believe. Not what they sell, not who they serve. What they actually think about the world their customers live in. That belief radiates through everything they make, and no style guide required.

So before you update your brand guidelines again, ask the harder question: does anyone in your organization know what this brand truly believes? If the answer is a hesitation, start there.

Authentic Leadership in the Age of AI: What Machines Can't Replicate

Every week I talk to leaders who are quietly wrestling with the same question: if AI can do the writing, the summarizing, the scheduling, the analyzing: what exactly is my job now?

It's a fair question. And the honest answer is that a significant portion of what used to fill a leader's calendar is being automated. Not someday. Now.

But here's what I keep coming back to: presence isn't a task. Trust isn't a deliverable. The willingness to sit in a hard conversation and stay human. To tell someone a difficult truth with care, to hold a team together when the strategy isn't working, to make a call that the data alone can't make. That's not being automated.

What AI is doing is clearing out the scaffolding. The reports, the drafts, the first passes. What's left, increasingly, is the part that only a person can do: judgment, relationship, accountability, and the courage to actually lead.

If you're a leader feeling unsettled by AI, I'd reframe the question. Not "what will I do if AI replaces my tasks?" but "now that AI is handling the tasks, what do I get to become?" In my experience, the answer is usually something closer to the leader you always meant to be.

Stop Chasing Trends. Start Building a Point of View.

There's a particular kind of exhaustion that comes from trying to keep up. A new platform, a new format, a new algorithm change, and the response is always the same: pivot, adapt, repeat. The brands playing this game are running hard and getting nowhere.

Meanwhile, a smaller set of brands is doing something different. They're not the first to adopt every new channel. They're not the loudest. But when they show up, people pay attention. The difference isn't budget, and it isn't reach. It's that they have something to say.

A genuine point of view is probably the most durable marketing asset a brand can build. It doesn't expire when the algorithm changes. It doesn't require a budget to spread. It's the thing that makes people feel like they found their people and want to stick around.

Building one takes honesty. You have to decide what you actually think, not just what your audience wants to hear. You have to be willing to be wrong about some things. And you have to resist the pressure to sand every edge until nothing sharp remains.

The brands I admire most aren't always right. But they're always themselves. In a world drowning in optimized content, that's rarer than you'd think, and more valuable than almost anything else.
